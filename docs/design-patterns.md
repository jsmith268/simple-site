# SimpleSight Design-Pattern Corpus

A working playbook of the *general patterns and principles* that separate a website that
looks professionally made from one that looks like it was assembled in an afternoon. None
of what follows describes any specific company, template, or proprietary layout. These are
craft principles — the same kind of knowledge a working designer carries between jobs.

## How to use this document

Treat this as inspiration fuel, not a stencil. Read the principles, internalize the
*reasons* behind them, and then author an original template that satisfies them. The
failure mode to avoid is the opposite of generic: do not study one admired site and
reproduce its structure, color choices, and section order. That produces a clone, and
clones look cheap precisely because the borrowed decisions don't fit the new business.

The right loop is: (1) read the per-industry section for the *feeling* and the
constraints, (2) make your own palette, type, and layout decisions inside those
constraints, (3) check the "amateur tells" list to make sure you haven't slipped into a
default. A pattern is a question — "what creates trust for a law firm?" — not an answer you
can copy.

## General principles of premium small-business web design

**Hierarchy is the whole game.** A visitor should be able to blur their eyes and still
know what the most important thing on the page is, then the second. Build that with three
or four dimensions at once — size, weight, color, and surrounding space — not size alone. A
headline that is merely bigger reads as timid; a headline that is bigger, heavier, and
floats in its own pool of whitespace reads as confident.

**Whitespace and rhythm signal expense.** Cramped pages feel discount; generous ones feel
considered. Pick a spacing scale and commit to it — a 4-point base (4, 8, 12, 16, 24, 32,
48, 64, 96) gives enough granularity without chaos. Tie vertical spacing to your
line-height so text and gaps share one mathematical foundation. The single fastest way to
make a layout look more expensive is to double the breathing room around the hero and
between sections.

**Pair type with intent, not reflex.** One well-chosen family in several weights usually
beats two competing fonts. When you do pair, contrast on a real axis — serif against sans,
condensed display against wide body — never two near-identical sans-serifs, which create
tension without hierarchy. Avoid the invisible defaults (Inter, Roboto, Open Sans, Lato,
Montserrat); they make a site feel like every other site. Choose the typeface whose
physical character matches *this* business.

**Keep the palette restrained.** Most small-business sites need exactly one accent color
plus a neutral scale. Accent works *because* it is rare — a 60/30/10 split of
neutral/secondary/accent by visual weight. Avoid pure gray and pure black; give neutrals a
faint chroma tilted toward the brand so surfaces and accent feel related. Reserve the
accent for the one action you most want taken.

**Use real photography, treated consistently.** A small set of genuine, well-lit photos —
even phone photos shot in good light — beats a pile of generic stock. Run every image
through the same treatment (a shared grade, a consistent crop ratio) so the set reads as
one voice rather than a scrapbook.

**One clear call to action per page.** Decide the single thing the page is for — book a
tour, request a valuation, view the menu — and make that the loudest interactive element.
Secondary actions get quieter, secondary styling.

**Design mobile-first and prove it.** Most of these visitors arrive on a phone. Stack
cleanly, keep tap targets at least 44px, never disable zoom, and confirm the layout holds
at 200%.

**Earn trust with concrete signals.** Real names, real faces, real addresses, real
testimonials with attribution, genuine credentials, a working phone number. Specificity is
credibility.

### Amateur tells to avoid

- **Emoji as icons.** Instantly reads as a hobby project. Use a single consistent icon set
  or no icons.
- **Stocky clip-art and the obvious stock photo** — the laughing-with-salad genre. If it
  could belong to any business, it belongs to none.
- **Rainbow palettes.** More than two or three hues with no clear roles looks like a child's
  toy box. Pick an accent and starve the rest.
- **Centering everything.** Centered body text and endlessly centered sections feel
  template-default and tire the eye. Left-align running text; use a real grid.
- **Default system-builder fonts at default sizes** with muddy, near-equal steps. Use fewer
  sizes with more contrast.
- **Dense walls of text.** Break into scannable chunks; cap measure near 65 characters.
- **Fake stats and invented badges.** "Trusted by 10,000+" with no basis, or
  award-looking graphics that mean nothing, destroy trust the moment they're doubted.
- **Drop shadows you can clearly see**, garish gradients, and three competing CTAs. If a
  shadow is obvious, it's too strong.

## Real estate

**Feeling to evoke:** calm confidence and aspiration. The visitor is imagining a life, and
also entrusting someone with the largest transaction of that life. The site should feel
both editorial — like a good property magazine — and reassuringly competent.

**Palette direction:** warm stone neutrals (sand, greige, soft limestone) with a single
restrained metallic or deep accent — brass, bronze, or a muted forest. Let property
photography supply the color; the interface stays quiet so listings pop. Avoid loud reds
and saturated blues, which read as discount-brokerage.

**Typography:** a refined transitional or humanist serif for headlines paired with a clean,
slightly wide sans for body and data. The serif lends heritage and taste; the sans keeps
prices, beds/baths, and addresses legible and orderly. Use tabular figures for numeric
specs.

**Imagery:** wide, bright, professionally composed interiors and exteriors; consistent
warm grade; one generous hero shot rather than a busy collage. Lifestyle and neighborhood
texture (a street, a cafe, morning light) used sparingly to sell place, not just property.

**Layout archetype:** an editorial, image-forward layout with a strong full-bleed hero and
roomy galleries. **Page set:** Home, Listings, single Listing detail, Neighborhoods,
About/Agent, Contact/Valuation. **Section order on the home page:** hero with one primary
CTA → featured listings → search or browse-by-area entry → neighborhoods → agent intro and
credibility → testimonials → valuation CTA → footer with contact.

**Must-have sections:** featured listings (with photo, price, key specs), full search or
filterable listing grid, agent bio with real credentials and contact, neighborhood guides,
and a "what's my home worth?" valuation call to action.

**What looks good:** big confident photography, lots of whitespace around price and specs,
a single accent on the booking/valuation CTA, real agent photos and a real phone number.
**What to avoid:** spinning house animations, multi-color "SOLD!" starbursts, cramped specs
crammed under tiny thumbnails, generic skyline stock that isn't the actual market.

## Law firm

**Feeling to evoke:** authority, discretion, and steadiness. A prospective client is
usually anxious and wants to feel they've found serious, careful people. The site should
feel measured and substantial — never flashy, never salesy.

**Palette direction:** deep navy or charcoal-ink as the anchor, paired with a warm metallic
or restrained gold accent and plenty of off-white space. Alternatives that still read as
serious: oxblood or deep forest as the anchor. Keep chroma low; gravity comes from depth
and contrast, not brightness.

**Typography:** a strong, traditional serif for headlines (this is one of the few verticals
where an "establishment" serif genuinely fits the brief) with a quiet, highly legible sans
for body. The contrast says *heritage plus clarity*. Generous line-height; nothing should
feel rushed.

**Imagery:** real attorney portraits shot consistently against a neutral ground; architectural
and city detail used as texture; documents and detail shots over cliche gavel/scales stock.
Treat all portraits with the same crop and grade so the team reads as a unit.

**Layout archetype:** a structured, grid-disciplined, content-forward layout that
prioritizes readability and credibility over imagery. **Page set:** Home, Practice Areas
(and a page per major area), Attorneys (and individual bios), Results/About, Contact with
consultation request. **Section order on the home page:** restrained hero with a clear
positioning statement and one CTA → practice areas → why-this-firm / credibility → attorney
intros → results or testimonials → consultation CTA → footer with office and contact.

**Must-have sections:** clearly organized practice areas, attorney bios with real
credentials (bar admissions, education, focus), trust signals (case results, recognitions,
client testimonials with attribution), and a prominent consultation call to action with a
phone number.

**What looks good:** disciplined typography, deep restrained color, real faces, specific
credentials, calm spacing that signals "we are not in a hurry to close you." **What to
avoid:** stock gavels and scale-of-justice clip-art, neon "FREE CONSULTATION!!!" banners,
invented success percentages, cramped multi-column text, and aggressive countdown-timer
urgency that undercuts the dignity the vertical requires.

## Bakery

**Feeling to evoke:** warmth, craft, and appetite. This should feel handmade and inviting —
the digital equivalent of a warm smell and a friendly counter. Personality is welcome here
in a way it isn't at a law firm.

**Palette direction:** cream and warm off-white base with a baked accent — rust, terracotta,
caramel, or a soft berry — and maybe one secondary warm tone. Earthy and food-friendly;
avoid cold blues and clinical grays, which suppress appetite.

**Typography:** a characterful display face for the name and headlines (a warm slab, a
friendly high-contrast serif, or a tasteful hand-influenced face used *only* at large
sizes) paired with a clean, readable body sans or humanist serif. Let the display face
carry the personality; keep menus and hours plain and legible.

**Imagery:** close, tactile, warmly lit food photography — crumb, crust, steam, texture —
plus a few honest shots of the space and the people. Natural light, shallow depth of field,
consistent warm grade. This is the one vertical where mouth-level close-ups do the selling.

**Layout archetype:** a warm, photo-rich, single-flow layout that can live comfortably on
one long page or a small set. **Page set (small):** Home, Menu, Gallery/Story, Visit/Order.
**Section order on the home page:** appetizing hero with the name and one CTA (order or
visit) → signature items / menu highlights → story / who-we-are → gallery → hours, location,
and order CTA → footer.

**Must-have sections:** the menu (clear, priced, scannable), a gallery of real bakes, a
short origin story, and unmistakable hours / location / order or contact info. For a bakery,
practical detail — when you're open, where you are, how to order — is as important as the
pretty pictures.

**What looks good:** big warm food photos, a friendly display face used sparingly, honest
shots of the space, clear hours and an easy order path. **What to avoid:** cold palettes,
emoji croissants and cupcakes as decoration, comic-style fonts everywhere, generic stock
pastries that aren't yours, and burying the hours or address three clicks deep.

## Yoga studio

**Feeling to evoke:** calm, space, and grounded ease. The visitor wants to feel that
stepping inside will lower their shoulders. Restraint *is* the brand here; clutter
contradicts the message.

**Palette direction:** a soft, low-chroma natural palette — sage, clay, oatmeal, warm
off-white — with one gentle accent (a muted terracotta or a deep eucalyptus). Sage-and-clay
calm. Everything desaturated; no high-energy primaries.

**Typography:** an airy, light-weight sans or a soft humanist serif, set large with generous
leading and open letter-spacing on headings. The type should feel like it's breathing. Avoid
anything heavy, condensed, or aggressive.

**Imagery:** serene, natural-light photography — real practitioners, real space, plants,
texture, negative space. Soft, slightly muted grade; movement and stillness over posed
perfection. Lots of empty room in the frame; the calm is in what you *don't* show.

**Layout archetype:** a spacious, generously-spaced, gallery-calm layout where whitespace is
the dominant element. **Page set:** Home, Schedule/Classes, Instructors, Pricing/Intro Offer,
About/Contact. **Section order on the home page:** quiet hero with one CTA (book a class or
claim the intro offer) → what-we-offer / class types → schedule preview → instructors →
pricing with intro offer → calm gallery → contact and CTA → footer.

**Must-have sections:** a clear class schedule, instructor bios with real photos and styles,
transparent pricing with an enticing intro offer for new students, and a calm gallery of the
space. The intro offer is the conversion engine of this vertical and deserves real
prominence.

**What looks good:** abundant whitespace, soft natural light, gentle desaturated color, a
single calm CTA, an obvious new-student intro offer. **What to avoid:** neon brights, busy
patterned backgrounds, hyper-flexible-pose stock that intimidates beginners, lotus and
namaste emoji as decoration, and crowded schedules with no breathing room.

## A closing rule

Every vertical above rewards the same discipline: pick the feeling, choose a restrained
palette and a deliberate type pairing to match it, lead with real photography treated
consistently, give the page room to breathe, and point everything at one clear action. Then
sanity-check against the amateur tells. The patterns are shared; the execution must be
original to the business in front of you.
